\section{Introduction}

Speech has moved from a transcription target into an operational interface. Clinical documentation reviews show that speech recognition is already used to create records whose content can affect workflow, cost, and downstream care processes \citep{hodgson2016risks,blackley2019speech}. Voice-driven applications similarly depend on ASR quality as users move from spoken intent to system action. In these settings, a small ambiguity around a decision-critical spoken detail can change whether a case is escalated, routed to routine review, or held for conservative manual assessment.

The ASR evaluation landscape has made important progress. WER and CER provide auditable transcript-similarity evidence. Semantic transcript metrics such as Semantic-WER and semantic distance connect ASR quality to meaning preservation and downstream usability \citep{roy2021semantic,kim2022semdist}. Selective prediction and reject-option work gives mature foundations for routing uncertainty toward abstention or human confirmation \citep{chow1970reject,angelopoulos2021conformal}. Together, these lines create a strong base for ASR systems that are accurate, meaningful, and uncertainty-aware.

High-stakes speech-driven workflows invite one more evaluation layer: decision stability. The useful question is whether plausible transcript alternatives around spoken decision atoms would preserve the downstream action. This paper focuses on atoms such as negation, amount, actor, action, time, intent, uncertainty, and scam pattern. A transcript can stay close under surface metrics while a plausible alternative around one of these atoms changes the operational decision.

We introduce Counterfactual Decision-Stability ASR (CDS-ASR), a downstream-aware ASR evaluation framework. CDS-ASR identifies risk atoms, constructs bounded plausible ASR counterfactual variants, scores decision instability with CEIS, and evaluates conservative recovery policies through aggregate replay. The motivating case is anti-fraud triage, but the method is framed as speech evaluation: it tests whether ASR hypotheses remain stable with respect to downstream actions.

The paper makes four contributions. First, it formulates decision-stability evaluation for ASR in high-stakes speech-to-decision workflows, shifting the evaluation target from transcript similarity to speech-to-action reliability. Second, it defines risk atoms as decision-critical transcript spans and uses plausible ASR alternatives to stress the downstream decision function. Third, it evaluates CEIS on a completed 300-row dual-reviewer audit surface with 900 model-level assessments per reviewer and reports strong reviewer agreement, including Cohen's kappa of 0.849970 for decision-change labels. Fourth, it ablates CEIS into full, no-atom-weight, no-plausibility, binary-atom, and top-3 aggregation variants, showing that decision-changing risk-atom instability is a stable evidence signal for conservative replay.

All empirical claims are tied to aggregate artifacts. Raw audio, raw transcripts, selected row identifiers, reviewer notes, and transcript-bearing runtime logs remain outside the public release boundary.
